### **1. Core Sales Motions: Inbound vs. Outbound**

In modern commercial operations, sales outreach is split into two primary motions:

* **Inbound Sales ("Incoming"):** Focuses on prospects who have actively expressed interest in your product or service.
  * **Triggers:** Website form fills, demo requests, whitepaper downloads, inbound chat, or pricing page visits.
  * **Key Priority (Speed to Lead):** Research shows prospects contacted within 5 minutes are significantly more likely to qualify. Inbound qualification agents (e.g., Fin for Sales, Qualified, Chili Piper) are often deployed to engage visitors in real time, qualify intent, and schedule meetings instantly.
* **Outbound Sales ("Outgoing"):** Proactive outreach to cold prospects who fit your Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) but have not yet expressed intent.
  * **Triggers:** Account research, trigger events (funding rounds, hiring spikes), and cold prospecting campaigns.
  * **Key Priority:** High-volume account intelligence, personalized cold messaging, and multi-channel cadence execution (email, phone, LinkedIn).
* **Full-Funnel Orchestration:** Hybrid model combining real-time inbound response with targeted account enrichment, automated lead nurture, and multi-channel outbound sequences.

---

### **2. Categories of Sales Calls**

As a prospect moves through the sales pipeline, calls shift from initial engagement to commercial deal execution:

1. **Cold Call:** Unsolicited telephone outreach to a target contact who has no prior relationship with your company. The goal is to hook attention in the first 10 seconds and secure a follow-up conversation.
2. **Warm Call / Inbound Response:** Contacting a prospect who engaged with an inbound touchpoint (e.g., downloaded a playbook or requested pricing details).
3. **Discovery Call:** The initial structured conversation designed to uncover the buyer's pain points, current technical stack, business goals, timeline, and decision-making team.
4. **Demo / Solution Pitch Call:** Presenting a tailored product walkthrough or proposal that directly maps your product features to the pain points uncovered during discovery.
5. **Proposal & Pricing Review Call:** Walking through commercial structures, discount guardrails, usage tiers, and contract scopes with key stakeholders.
6. **Negotiation & Legal Review Call:** Resolving contract redlines, payment terms, regulatory compliance requirements, and commercial terms prior to deal sign-off.
7. **Closing Call:** The final touchpoint to review signed contracts, confirm order readiness, and transition the account to customer success or onboarding teams.

### **4. What to Know Before Making Calls (Pre-Call Preparation)**

Before picking up the phone or joining a video call, salespeople must prepare using structured qualification frameworks such as **BANT**:

* **Budget:**
  * **What to know:** Does the prospect have dedicated capital allocated for this problem? What are their financial parameters or pricing tier expectations?
  * **Why it matters:** Proposing enterprise tiers to a customer with small-business budget constraints leads to immediate deal stall or severe margin degradation.
* **Authority:**
  * **What to know:** Who is the economic buyer, who holds final sign-off authority, and who are the internal champions or technical evaluators?
  * **Why it matters:** Complex enterprise deals involve multiple stakeholders (IT, procurement, legal, executive sponsors); failing to map influence networks delays closing.
* **Need:**
  * **What to know:** What specific operational friction, revenue loss, or technical gap is the customer attempting to eliminate?
* **Timeline & Urgency:**
  * **What to know:** Is there a compelling business event (quarter-end, contract expiry, regulatory change) driving the target go-live date?

#### **Pre-Call Preparation Checklist:**
1. **Account Context Check:** Review prior interactions, email logs, past support tickets, or installed equipment in the CRM.
2. **Firmographic & Tech Stack Research:** Know their industry, company headcount, recent news, and existing software integrations.
3. **Objective Setting:** Define the single concrete outcome desired for the call (e.g., securing a discovery follow-up, confirming budget approval thresholds, or scheduling technical review).

### **5. Strategies for Sales Role-Playing**

For beginners, scenario-based role-playing is the fastest way to build muscle memory and confidence:

1. **Simulate Specific Call Scenarios:** Practice specific stages independently (e.g., 30-second cold call openers, handling harsh budget rejections, or steering discovery calls) rather than generic end-to-end pitches.
2. **Master Objection Handling Frameworks:**
   * **LAER Framework:** *Listen* (let the prospect speak without interrupting), *Acknowledge* (validate their concern), *Explore* (ask clarifying questions to uncover the root issue), *Respond* (deliver a concise, value-backed answer).
   * **Feel-Felt-Found:** Empathize with their concern, share how similar clients felt initially, and explain the positive outcome those clients found.
3. **AI-Led & Avatar Roleplay Simulators:** Utilize AI video or voice platforms (e.g., 1mind or conversational simulators) to practice cold calls and objection handling in a low-stakes, highly repeatable environment.
4. **Record and Analyze Conversations:** Review call recordings to evaluate metrics such as talk-to-listen ratio (aim for 40% talking / 60% listening), filler word frequency, and question depth.

### **10. Compliance Basics**

An outbound agent that ignores these rules is a real legal risk. This is a checklist for you to verify with counsel, not legal advice:

* **Email consent and opt-out:** requirements vary by region (for example CAN-SPAM in the US, GDPR and ePrivacy in the EU and UK). Every outbound email needs a working opt-out, and opt-outs must be honored across all sequences.
* **Call recording consent:** some jurisdictions require every party to consent. Announce recording at the start of a call.
* **Do-not-call lists:** check them before cold calling.
* **Personal data:** keep only what you need, and know where it is stored. Local models keep prompts and drafts on your machine, but enrichment and CRM tools may not.

### **Agentic Workflow**

Here is the agentic workflow for everything around the call, in the order a deal moves. It's built as several separate Dify workflows that share the CRM as their common state, rather than one large graph. Each is small enough to test and to pause for approval on its own.

## The flow at a glance

```
Lead arrives -> Enrich + score -> Route -> First reply/outreach -> (nurture loop)
   -> Meeting booked -> Pre-call brief -> [ LIVE CALL, human ]
   -> Transcript summary -> CRM update + next steps -> Follow-up draft
   -> Proposal/quote -> Negotiation support -> Close handoff
```

Dify has no cross-run memory of its own, so the CRM is the shared state. Each workflow reads a deal's record, does its job, and writes back. A schedule or webhook trigger starts the next one.

## Stage by stage

**1. Lead intake (inbound)** *[stock]*
- Trigger: webhook from the form or chat, or a polling schedule.
- Steps: dedupe against the CRM (HTTP lookup), then create or update the contact.
- No approval needed. It's a mechanical write, but make it idempotent.

**2. Enrichment and scoring** *[config; build for real enrichment APIs]*
- Call an enrichment API for company, headcount, tech stack and news.
- The LLM scores the lead against your ICP and fills BANT fields as *known / unknown / guessed*. Keep guesses labelled as guesses so nobody mistakes them for facts.
- IF/ELSE routes on score: high goes to a rep, medium goes to nurture, low is archived.

**3. Routing** *[stock logic, build for owner routing]*
- Assign by territory, size or round robin, and notify the owner (Slack or email). Owner-aware approval routing is the Phase 3 build item, so until then approvals go to one person.

**4. First reply or outreach** *[stock, the Phase 1 workflow]*
- Retrieve playbook and pricing from the knowledge base, draft the message, then a **Human Input** gate (approve, edit, reject).
- Only the approved branch sends. This is where approval and edit rate become your quality metric.
- Outbound prospecting uses the same graph with a schedule trigger and an iteration over a lead list. Add a suppression-list check (opt-outs, do-not-contact) before drafting.

**5. Nurture loop** *[stock]*
- A schedule trigger finds leads with no reply after N days, drafts a follow-up with new value (not "just checking in"), and gates it.
- Cap the number of touches and stop on any reply or opt-out.

**6. Meeting scheduling** *[config]*
- Offer slots through a calendar integration (HTTP or MCP). Booking should be a tool call with approval on the first send only, once you trust it.

**7. Pre-call brief** *[stock, read-only]*
- Triggered by the booked meeting. It pulls CRM history, past tickets and recent news, retrieves relevant playbook sections, and outputs the BANT gaps, one call objective and three discovery questions.
- Deliver it to the rep before the call. No approval gate because nothing is written.

## After the call

**8. Transcript to CRM** *[config; speech-to-text is separate]*
- Transcribe locally, then the LLM extracts summary, BANT updates, objections, commitments and next steps as structured output.
- A **Human Input** gate shows a before/after of the proposed CRM changes. This is the stage that most needs the review UI I flagged as a [build] item, because a plain text form makes diffs hard to check. Never let it write BANT fields or stage changes unreviewed.

**9. Follow-up email** *[stock]*
- Draft a recap with the agreed next steps, gate it, send, and log the activity.

**10. Proposal and quote** *[build for CPQ integration]*
- The agent assembles a proposal from templates and pricing in the knowledge base. Hard rules live in code, not the prompt: a discount over a threshold forces a manager approval node, and the LLM never sets a price.

**11. Negotiation support** *[stock]*
- Summarize redlines, flag non-standard terms against your policy docs, and draft responses. All outputs are suggestions for a human, since legal and commercial commitments should never be automated.

**12. Close and handoff** *[config]*
- On "closed won", create the onboarding task, notify customer success, and send an internal summary. On "closed lost", require a reason before the stage changes.

## Cross-cutting pieces

- **Approval inbox:** with this many gates, one person reviewing from separate email links won't scale. The inbox with assignees and bulk approve is the Phase 3 build. Until then, group gates to keep the volume down.
- **Stop conditions:** every loop (nurture, outreach) needs a hard cap and an opt-out check.
- **Audit trail:** log who approved what and the exact text sent. This is also a [build] item.
- **Failure handling:** if the CRM write or an API call fails, route to a human instead of retrying blindly. n8n helps here if retries get complicated.
- **Local vs cloud:** drafting, scoring and summaries can run on your local model. Enrichment, email sending, calendar and the CRM are external, so privacy covers reasoning only.

## What the canvas already does (stock)

The editor is a ReactFlow node canvas (`web/app/components/workflow/`).
- **Add steps:** drag from the block selector, or use the "+" on an edge. This covers LLM, HTTP, tool, code, knowledge retrieval, IF/ELSE, loop and the others.
- **Remove or modify steps:** delete a node, edit its panel, or rewire edges.
- **Add an approval step:** drop in a Human Input node and define its form and action buttons. Each button becomes an output edge.
- **Test and iterate:** single-node run, variable inspect, draft versus published versions, and DSL import/export.

So for you as a builder, ComfyUI-style editing works today. Where it falls short is for a sales ops person who shouldn't need to know about nodes, edges or selectors.

## Three ways to get a friendlier editor

Each option is more work than the last.

**Option A: Use the canvas with templates (days, no code).**
- Ship your workflows as DSL templates (like the two I generated) and a short guide: "to require approval before step X, insert a Human Input node after it and wire Approve to the next step."
- Cost: the user has to wire the Reject and timeout edges by hand each time.

**Option B: A thin stage-level editor on top of Dify (build, medium).**
- A small web app shows your pipeline as a list of stages (intake, score, draft, send, write to CRM) with controls: add, remove, reorder, edit prompt, and a **"Require approval" toggle per step**.
- It edits the workflow through Dify's console API. I confirmed the draft endpoint exists (`/apps/<id>/workflows/draft`, plus publish). When a toggle is turned on, the app inserts a Human Input node after that step and wires the Approve, Reject and timeout edges. When it's turned off, it removes the node and reconnects the neighbors.
- Advanced users can still open the full canvas.
- Risks: it depends on Dify's internal draft graph format, which can change between versions. It also needs auth handling and tests against the import validation. I haven't built or tried it.

**Option C: Make approval a property of every node (build, larger, in Dify's core).**
- Add a `require_approval` setting to the shared node panel so any node can say "pause here and ask a human before running". Behind the scenes it creates the form and the edges, which keeps the canvas clean.
- It touches the base node config, the runtime, the frontend panel and probably the human-input pause handling (`api/core/workflow/nodes/human_input/`). It's the cleanest result, but it forks Dify's behavior and complicates upgrades.

## Policy-based approval (an addition to B or C)

For CRM, "approval needed" often depends on the data, not the step. Examples: discount over 10 percent, a new contact, a message to an executive. A rules table (condition then approver) can be evaluated by an IF/ELSE ahead of a Human Input node. A rules editor in Option B's UI could generate that routing. This is one of the highest-value additions for sales use.

## My recommendation

1. **Now:** do Option A. It costs nothing and shows whether people actually want to change steps.
2. **Next:** build Option B, focusing on the per-step approval toggle and a rules table. It delivers most of the ComfyUI feel without forking Dify.
3. **Later:** only do Option C if you find that Option B's graph editing keeps breaking across Dify upgrades.

Whichever you pick, the approval inbox and audit log from the roadmap matter more as you add gates.

## What it is

The Agents page is backed by the `dify-agent` service in this repo (the `agent_backend` container in your stack). Its README describes the runtime as hosting Pydantic AI runs behind FastAPI. You build an agent by assembling layers:

| Layer | What it does |
|---|---|
| `prompt` | The agent's standing instructions and the user message |
| `llm` | The model, chosen from your installed model plugins |
| `tools` | Tools from your plugins that the agent can call |
| `knowledge` | Retrieval from your knowledge bases |
| `history` | Conversation memory. A run can resume from a saved session. |
| `output` | Structured output, so results come back in a fixed shape |
| `shell` | A command-line workspace in a sandbox, for tasks that need files or commands |

The workflow canvas has a matching **Agent** block (the `agent-v2` node in the repo). It lets a workflow hand one step to an agent.
